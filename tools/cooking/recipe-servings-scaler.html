Recipe Servings Scaler: Make Any Recipe Fit Your Table

Every home cook has hit the moment: a beloved recipe written for four, and six hungry guests arriving in an hour. Scaling by hand means doing the arithmetic twice for every ingredient - and usually rounding it differently each time. This recipe servings scaler does the whole list in one pass, with rounding that actually makes sense in the kitchen.

How to Use the Recipe Servings Scaler

  1. Paste or type your ingredients, one per line, starting with the amount - try "2 cups flour", "250 g butter", "1 1/2 tsp salt".
  2. Set the original servings the recipe is written for.
  3. Set the target servings for your table.
  4. Read off the scaled list - the scale factor is shown up top, and every amount is rounded to two significant figures for easy measuring.

The tool understands whole numbers, decimals and fractions like "1/2" and "1 1/2", and it preserves the unit and ingredient on every line.

The Math Behind the Scaling

Scaling is one multiplication: scale factor = target servings / original servings. Every ingredient amount is multiplied by that factor. A recipe for 4 scaled to 6 uses a factor of 1.5, so 250 g of butter becomes 375 g. Amounts are then rounded to two significant figures - 7.333 cups becomes a practical 7.4 cups.

Once your ingredient amounts are right, set the temperature correctly with the Oven Temperature Converter, and plan how long a larger or smaller roast needs with the Cooking Time Calculator.

Scaling Tips That Save Dinner

  • Round the final amount, not the factor - measuring 7.4 cups is easy; cooking "one and a third of a pinch less" is not.
  • Leave spices alone when scaling up strongly - double or triple a chili recipe and you may want 1.5× the spice, not 3×.
  • Expect a longer cook in a fuller tray - a larger casserole holds heat differently than a small one.
  • Watch thin batters - scaling up can overflow a tin faster than you expect; use a bigger tin.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I scale a recipe for more servings?

Divide the servings you want by the servings the recipe makes to get a scale factor, then multiply every ingredient amount by that factor. A recipe for 4 scaled to 6 means multiplying every amount by 1.5.

Does scaling change cooking time?

Often, yes. A larger tray or batch usually cooks a little longer, while smaller batches cook faster. Scaling the ingredients is a great first step, but always check doneness by temperature and look, not just by time.

Why do scaled amounts look odd?

Raw multiplication often produces amounts like 7.333 cups of flour. This tool rounds to two significant figures so you get practical amounts like 7.4 cups, which are far easier to measure, instead of fussy decimals.
