Note Frequency Calculator: Bridge the Gap Between Notes and Hertz

When a synth patch needs to sit exactly on a note, or a plugin asks for a frequency that matches your key, the fastest way is plain math. This note frequency calculator converts between musical notes and exact frequencies in both directions, so you can tune instruments, equalizers, and resonant filters without breaking creative flow.

How to Use the Note Frequency Calculator

  1. Note to Hz: choose C–B, add a sharp or flat, then pick the octave. The exact frequency appears instantly along with its MIDI number.
  2. Hz to Note: type any frequency in hertz and read back the nearest note, its octave, the cents offset, and the matching MIDI number.
  3. Copy the result for your patch notes or channel list. The whole scale from C0 to B8 is shown at the bottom as a reference.

Try it: A4 returns 440 Hz, C4 (middle C) returns 261.63 Hz, and A5 returns exactly 880 Hz.

The Frequency Formula

Across all 12 notes of the octave, every semitone is the same ratio, the 12th root of two. That makes the math clean: f = 440 × 2^((MIDI − 69)/12), where MIDI 69 is A4. Going backwards, MIDI = 69 + 12 × log₂(f/440), rounded to the nearest whole number for the closest note, with the cents offset computed from the remaining distance (1 cent = 1/100 of a semitone).

Why Note Frequencies Matter in Production

Equalizers and filters cut at frequencies, not note names. A kick drum centred on a strong root-note frequency presses harder against a mix, and a vocal de-esser targets a specific resonant band near 4–8 kHz. Musicians tuning percussion, bell sounds, and bass lines by ear often miss by several cents; this calculator removes the guesswork.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert a musical note to a frequency?

Find the note's MIDI number, then use f = 440 × 2^((MIDI - 69)/12). MIDI 69 is A4 at 440 Hz. Raising the MIDI number by 12 doubles the frequency, so each octave is exactly twice the previous one.

What does the cents offset tell me?

A cent is one hundredth of a semitone. The calculator rounds to the nearest note of the 12-tone scale, and the cents value shows how far off that note the input frequency really is. Fifty cents is exactly halfway to the next semitone.

What tuning does this calculator use?

12-tone equal temperament (12-TET) with A4 tuned to the standard concert pitch of 440 Hz, the international reference most instruments and DAWs use.
