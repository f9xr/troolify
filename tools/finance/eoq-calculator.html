EOQ Calculator Guide: Formula, Reorder Point and Cost Breakdown

The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) model answers one question better than any other in inventory management: how many units should you buy each time you place an order? Order too little and you pay a fortune in purchase-order admin and emergency freight. Order too much and your cash disappears into a warehouse. EOQ finds the single order size where those two costs balance, so your total inventory cost lands at its lowest possible point. This guide explains the theory behind the model, how the formula is derived, and how to apply it to real purchasing decisions.

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ): Theory and Application

Inventory carries two competing costs. Each time you raise a purchase order you pay a ordering cost - the time and money spent raising it, obtaining approval, negotiating, receiving and inspecting. Every unit that sits in your warehouse meanwhile costs you a holding cost: the rent and utilities per square foot, insurance, financing the money that is tied up in stock, and the slow erosion of value through spoilage, damage or obsolescence.

Order in small batches and the ordering cost soars. Order in large batches and the holding cost soars. The Economic Order Quantity model sits precisely at the balance point between the two, and it does so with a single formula:

EOQ = √(2DS / H)

where D is annual demand in units, S is the cost of raising one order, and H is the cost of holding one unit for one year. Square-root relationships are the signature of EOQ: because demand, ordering cost and holding cost all sit inside a square root, the model is forgiving of imperfect inputs. Doubling your estimated ordering cost changes the EOQ by only about 41%, and halving it changes the EOQ by only about 29%. In practice that robustness is what makes EOQ so widely trusted.

Where the formula comes from

The derivation is short and worth understanding, because it explains every assumption baked into the result. If you order a quantity Q each time:

  • The number of orders you place per year is D / Q, so your annual ordering cost is (D / Q) × S. This falls as Q rises.
  • Stock drains from Q down to zero on average, so your average holding is Q / 2 units and your annual holding cost is (Q / 2) × H. This rises as Q rises.

Adding the two gives total annual inventory cost TC(Q) = (D/Q)S + (Q/2)H. The EOQ is the point where the derivative of that expression is zero, which gives Q* = √(2DS / H). At that quantity the ordering cost and the holding cost are exactly equal - a useful sanity check you can apply to any EOQ result, and one this calculator displays for you.

What EOQ assumes

EOQ is a model, and it earns its keep by being simple rather than exhaustive. It assumes demand is steady and known, lead times are constant, and quantity discounts do not apply. It ignores stock-out penalties, production set-up times, and the reality that suppliers quote a minimum order quantity. Where those matter you adjust rather than abandon: order in multiples of the supplier’s pack size, add a safety-stock buffer, and treat the output as a strong starting point to be validated against real purchase-order history rather than an instruction to be obeyed blindly.

Applying EOQ in practice

Estimate D from last year’s actual issues or sales, not from a forecast. Estimate S as the full cost of one order - staff time, freight, receiving, inspection - not just the cost of the purchase order itself, because underestimating S is the single most common error and it pushes the EOQ too low. Estimate H as a percentage of unit value (commonly 18-25% of unit cost per year) if you do not have a hard number. Then run the EOQ, round it to something you can actually order, and compare the modelled saving against your current practice. This calculator also reports the reorder point, the maximum stock level and how sensitive the cost is to being near - but not exactly at - the optimum.

How Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) Works

Once you have an EOQ, four numbers let you run the whole cycle: the order quantity itself, the reorder point, the cycle length and the maximum stock level. Here is the working, step by step.

1. Order the EOQ

Place each purchase order for the calculated EOQ quantity. Round to the nearest practical figure - a full pallet, a full case, or the supplier’s minimum order quantity.

2. Find the reorder point

You cannot reorder when stock runs out, because you still have to wait out the lead time. The reorder point is the stock level that triggers the next order:

Reorder point = (Daily demand × Lead time in days) + Safety stock

Daily demand is annual demand divided by your working days. Safety stock is the buffer that protects you against demand spikes and late deliveries. With the numbers in this calculator - 12,000 units a year, 250 working days, a 7-day lead time and 200 units of safety stock - you reorder when stock falls to 536 units.

3. Work out the cycle length

The cycle length is how long one order lasts before you need the next one:

Cycle length (days) = Working days ÷ Orders per year

With an EOQ of 400 units against 12,000 units of annual demand you raise 30 orders a year, so each order covers roughly 8.3 working days of stock. Set your review calendar to match that cadence.

4. Set the maximum stock level

The maximum stock level is the highest point in the cycle - it is what you hold immediately after a delivery arrives:

Max stock level = EOQ + Safety stock

5. Check the cost

Total annual inventory cost is the ordering cost plus the holding cost. At the EOQ these two are equal, so each is half the total - a quick check that your inputs are sane. The curve is flat near the minimum: in the sensitivity table below, ordering 25% more or less than the EOQ changes the annual cost by well under 1%, so an EOQ rounded to a practical number costs you almost nothing.

6. Re-run it when the business changes

EOQ is a snapshot. Demand that grows, an inflation-driven rise in holding costs, or a supplier move to smaller minimum orders all shift the answer. Re-run the model each planning cycle and compare it with actual purchase orders - if your real ordering cost is higher than you assumed, the EOQ should be lower than you currently order.

Tips for getting accurate results

  • Include freight, receiving and inspection in the ordering cost - excluding them is the most common cause of an EOQ that is too low.
  • Estimate holding cost as 18-25% of unit value per year when you have no hard number.
  • Use real demand history rather than a forecast; EOQ is only as good as D.
  • Round the EOQ up to a practical order size, then check the sensitivity table - small deviations cost very little.
  • Re-run the model whenever demand, supplier terms or storage costs change materially.

Key takeaways

  • EOQ balances ordering cost against holding cost by taking the square root of 2DS/H.
  • At the optimum, ordering cost and holding cost are exactly equal.
  • The reorder point is where you place the next order - not where stock runs out.
  • The cost curve is flat near the optimum, so a practical rounded order size costs almost nothing.
  • EOQ is a strong starting point that still needs validating against real purchase orders.

Frequently asked questions

What does EOQ stand for?

Economic Order Quantity - the order size that minimises the total annual cost of ordering and holding stock.

How do I calculate EOQ?

Use EOQ = √(2DS / H): D is annual demand in units, S is the cost of one order, and H is the annual holding cost per unit.

What is the difference between EOQ and the reorder point?

The EOQ is how many units to buy each time; the reorder point is the stock level that triggers the next order (daily demand × lead time + safety stock).

Should I order exactly the EOQ?

Usually not exactly - round up to a pack, case or supplier minimum. The cost curve is flat near the optimum, so a practical quantity is nearly as good.

What if my demand changes through the year?

EOQ assumes steady demand. Run it on an average or on seasonal profiles, and re-run it as demand changes.

Does EOQ account for quantity discounts?

No - the basic model assumes a constant unit price. With discounts, compare the total cost at each price break rather than the EOQ alone.

Is my data sent anywhere?

No. Every calculation runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded or stored.

What is a good holding cost percentage?

Most businesses land between 18% and 25% of unit value per year, depending on storage cost, insurance and financing.
