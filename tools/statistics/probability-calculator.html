Probability Calculator: Six Questions, Counted Properly

Probability questions look varied but almost all of them reduce to one habit: count the outcomes you want, count all the outcomes, and divide. The tricky part is always getting the second count right. This calculator handles the six most common versions so you can check both counts and see where the answers come from.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Choose the question type - simple event, two dice, coin tosses, combinations, permutations or at least one.
  2. Enter the numbers. The labels change to match the type.
  3. Read the probability as a fraction, a percentage and a one-in-N chance.

Simple Events and the Sample Space

For equally likely outcomes the probability is favourable divided by total. A fair die has six faces, so a 3 is one in six. The assumption matters: biased coins and weighted dice break the simple ratio, which is why real experiments rarely match the textbook answer.

Two Dice and Sums

Two standard dice give 36 equally likely combinations, and each total has a fixed number of ways to make it. Sums of 7 and 2 are the extremes of the distribution - 7 has six combinations, 2 and 12 have just one. The bell-shaped pattern is the same shape as any sum of many random steps, which is why averages cluster in the middle.

Combinations and Permutations

These answer "how many ways" rather than "what is the chance". A combination is a selection where order does not matter, so picking 2 people from 5 is the same group however you counted them - written as n choose r. A permutation is an ordered arrangement, so first place and second place are different, giving n! over (n-r)!. The classic mistake is using one when you need the other: passwords and race finishing orders are permutations, lottery draws and picking teammates are combinations.

The "At Least One" Trick

Calculating the chance of at least one success directly means adding up lots of overlapping cases, so almost nobody does it that way. Instead, take the complement: the chance of at least one head is 1 minus the chance of no heads at all. For five fair flips that is 1 - 0.5⁵ = 96.875%, and the complement method turns a hard sum into one multiplication.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate basic probability?

Divide the number of favourable outcomes by the total number of equally likely outcomes. Rolling a 3 on a six-sided die is 1 in 6, which is about 16.67%.

What is the probability of rolling a certain sum with two dice?

Two dice have 36 equally likely combinations. Sums of 7 and 2 are the most likely with 6 combinations each, while 2 and 12 are the least likely with 1 each. The probability of any sum is its combination count divided by 36.

How does the coin toss calculator work?

It uses the binomial distribution. The chance of getting exactly k heads in n fair coin flips is the number of ways to choose which k flips land heads, written as n choose k, multiplied by 0.5 to the power n. It also gives the chance of at least one head, which is the complement of all tails.

Conclusion

Probability gets intuitive once you see the same fraction appearing in a dice roll and a coin toss. Count the outcomes, count the wins, divide. That single habit answers most exam questions, and this calculator is there to confirm your count every time.
