Nominal tonnage is a rating-bracket conversion (12,000 BTU/h per ton). Real units derate with ambient temperature, so the nominal value is a comparison number, not a deliverable promise. Full disclaimer.

The 12,000 BTU/h ton

One nominal refrigeration ton is the cooling effect of melting a ton of ice over 24 hours - standardized as 12,000 BTU/h. When a condenser data sheet says 3 tons, it means 36,000 BTU/h at rated conditions, not necessarily exactly 3.00 tons of delivered cooling.

Brackets, not verdicts

Residential split systems commonly step in 0.5- and 1-ton increments (1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 5). Converting a design load to tons simply picks the closest bracket; matching a load to exactly a brochure ton is coincidental.

Real-world derating

Rated capacity holds at the AHRI condition point (95 F outdoor). Hotter ambients reduce delivered capacity, so a unit selected at exactly the design load can be short during the design-day peak. Convert, then add margin.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many BTU/h are in 1 ton?

12,000 BTU/h exactly. In SI, that is 3.51685 kW of thermal capacity.

Why are ACs advertised as 2 or 3 ton but called by tons at all?

Because early refrigeration was literally rated by the ice it could make. The tonnage unit stuck and now simply means 12,000 BTU/h blocks of cooling capacity.

Does 2 tons always deliver 24,000 BTU/h?

Only at rated conditions. At higher ambients or with line losses, delivered capacity drops below the nominal rating - design for the worst case.

Can I use tons directly from a load calculation?

You can convert it (12,000 BTU/h per ton), but select the equipment on BTU/h at the design condition and check the derated curve, not the nominal tons alone.

Conclusion

Turning BTU/h into tons is trivial arithmetic; using the bracket responsibly is the actual job. Convert, compare, and add margin for the design-day.
