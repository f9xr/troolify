Why rectangular

Rectangular ducts squeeze large cross-sections into wall and ceiling cavities where round duct cannot fit. The trade-off is higher friction per unit area for the same capacity, which is why aspect ratio is kept modest - 4:1 is a common practical ceiling.

Defining width and height

With area fixed (CFM / velocity), the aspect ratio fixes the pair: height = √(area / ratio) and width = ratio × height. A wider duct is shorter and shallower; too wide a ratio means more surface resistance for less material benefit.

Even-inch rounding

Sheet metal shops fabricate in even-inch steps, so the raw inches are rounded up to even sizes. Rounding up adds area, which drops the actual velocity slightly and tunes down friction.

The equal-friction bridge

Comparing a rectangular duct to a round one uses the ASHRAE equal-friction formula - the tool reports the equivalent round diameter so you can carry the size into the round friction loss check without guessing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good aspect ratio for rectangular duct?

Between 1:1 and 4:1. Higher ratios fit tight spaces but add friction and installation labour per square inch of area.

How do I convert rectangular to round duct?

Use the ASHRAE equal-friction formula: 1.30 × (ab)^0.625 / (a+b)^0.25. The tool reports this automatically.

Is a square duct better than a flat one?

Friction-wise yes - a square (1:1) has the least surface for a given area. Flat ducts trade efficiency for installation clearance.

Should I size to the exact CFM?

Size to the design CFM rounded up to standard sizes. Oversizing rect duct modestly is safer than undersizing because deflection and noise grow quickly under 400 fpm constraints get tight.

Conclusion

Rectangular sizing is area math pinned by an aspect ratio. Keep the ratio sane, round to even inches, and bridge friction through the equivalent round diameter.
