Exhausted air is unconditioned air

Every CFM a fume hood rejects, another must be conditioned and supplied. In an air-conditioned lab, make-up air for the exhaust is one of the largest energy costs on the mechanical bill.

The 4.5 rule

For dry standard air, the heat content of moving air is 4.5 × CFM × enthalpy change in Btu/lb (4.5 = 0.075 lb/ft³ × 60 min/h). Multiply the exhaust airflow by the enthalpy gap between supply and room, and the load in BTU/h appears.

Why enthalpy, not temperature

Make-up air load is mostly latent in humid regions - a warm, moist air slide onto a dry room. Enthalpy captures moisture and temperature together, which is why the tool asks for enthalpies from a psychrometric chart rather than just a thermostat reading.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate make-up air load?

Multiply the exhaust airflow (CFM) by 4.5 times the enthalpy difference between supply and room air in Btu/lb. The result is BTU/h.

Why is lab exhaust load so expensive?

Because the air is discarded after one pass - every conditioned CFM is thrown away and replaced. High ACH and hood exhaust multiply that once-through cost.

What enthalpy should I use?

The supply-air state and the exhaust/room-air state from a psychrometric chart at your altitude. The Psychrometric Calculator produces both from dry-bulb and RH.

Does the formula include makeup heating in winter?

Yes - the enthalpy difference works both ways: cold dry supply raises the load in heating season just as humid supply does in cooling season.

Conclusion

The exhaust load is the true cost of lab ventilation. Compute CFM from a documented scenario and enthalpy from measured states, and the energy bill stops surprising you.
