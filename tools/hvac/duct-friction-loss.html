Straight Round Duct Friction

Ready - straight-run friction estimate in this tab.

Straight-run round-duct estimate for standard galvanized steel at ~20 C air. Fittings, transitions, dampers and flexible duct add losses the straight-run formula does not cover. Full disclaimer.

Why friction is the second sizing question

A duct sized by geometry moves air, but only if the fan can overcome the friction it creates. Pressure drop per run is the bill the fan pays, and straight-round runs are the easiest bill to compute transparently - which is exactly what this tool does.

The method

Velocity and Reynolds number feed the Swamee-Jain friction factor for the stated roughness, and Darcy-Weisbach converts it to inches of water gauge across the duct length. Every intermediate - velocity, Re, f - is printed so the result is auditable.

Straight-run honesty

The formula models clean, continuous round duct. Elbows, tees, transitions, dampers, flex and internal fittings all add velocity- and geometry-dependent losses; a full EL (equivalent-length) accounting is the next step for real systems.

Typical friction targets

Common design targets are 0.08-0.12 in w.g. per 100 ft for low-pressure systems. Compare the per-100-ft output against your design constant to see if the selected diameter is in budget.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good friction rate for duct design?

Low-pressure systems usually target 0.08-0.12 in w.g. per 100 ft of straight duct; higher friction rates suit space-constrained industrial runs with more fan capacity.

What is in w.g.?

Inches of water gauge - the HVAC pressure unit. 1 in w.g. equals 249.09 pascals, roughly the pressure of an inch-deep column of water.

Why is flexible duct lossier?

Its corrugated interior creates turbulence; roughness of 0.9 mm is about ten times galvanized steel, raising the friction factor and static pressure cost.

Can I add fittings to this?

Not directly - fittings are equivalent-length additions (each elbow/branch risks several feet of extra loss). Add their equivalent lengths and rerun the straight formula.

Conclusion

Straight-run friction is physics with no hiding. Compute it at the real length and roughness, keep the per-100-ft rate in budget, and the fan selection will thank you.
