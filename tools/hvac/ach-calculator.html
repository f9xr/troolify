CFM to Air Changes

Ready - CFM to ACH in this tab.

The implied ACH is only as valid as the CFM figure behind it. Compare against the code target for the space type before changing fan setup. Full disclaimer.

The reverse check

Equally important as sizing a fan to hit an ACH target is checking what ACH an existing fan actually delivers. Divide the delivered CFM by the room volume and multiply by 60 - the result is the honest exchange rate of the space.

Reading the number

Home general ventilation commonly lands between 1 and 4 ACH, kitchens and baths 6-10 during use, and lab exhaust systems frequently require 8-12 or more by code. The comfortable band here is only a flag - the code for the space wins.

CFM is the assumption

If the CFM comes from a fan curve rather than a pitot measurement, treat the ACH as theoretical maximum, not measured performance. Filters, duct bends and dampers all eat static pressure and turn the curve value into something smaller.

Tying it to sizing

Work this tool in reverse with the CFM Calculator: set the ACH you want, get the CFM you need, then size ductwork with the Duct Size Calculator.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is ACH calculated from CFM?

Multiply CFM by 60 to get cubic feet per hour, then divide by the room volume in cubic feet.

What ACH is normal for a bedroom?

Around 1-2 ACH for comfort. Higher rates are used in kitchens, bathrooms, and any space with odour or moisture demands.

Does fan rating equal delivered airflow?

No - the fan curve gives CFM against static pressure. Restrictive ductwork and filters reduce delivered airflow below the free-air rating.

Why does my lab need 10+ ACH?

Lab exhaust codes (e.g. ASHRAE 170, NFPA 45) set minimum ACH for contaminant dilution and hood capture, which far exceed home ventilation rates.

Conclusion

The ACH implied by a CFM figure is the single best check that your fan actually exchanges the air your design promised.
