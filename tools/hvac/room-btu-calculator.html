Area-Based Room BTU

Ready - area-based estimate in this tab.

A rule-of-thumb area estimate, not a load calculation. Solar gain, window orientation, occupancy patterns and construction details can change the real load by more than 30%. Full disclaimer.

A documented starting number

Most room-sizing questions start with square footage. The industry shorthand is roughly 20 BTU/h per square foot for a typical conditioned room, so this calculator begins there - then documents every adjustment it applies so the reasoning behind the final number stays visible.

Adjustments and why they exist

Taller rooms hold more air to condition (+5% per extra foot above 8 ft), sunny exposures add solar gain, poor insulation leaks sensible heat, hot-humid climates add latent load, and each steady occupant contributes heat. Each factor is a documented percentage or rate, not a hidden fudge.

Picking a unit size

Once you have the BTU/h estimate, match the closest common window or mini-split size. Convert to tons with the BTU to Tons tool and compare with the BTU per Square Foot tool when a stated capacity needs a sanity check.

The limits of area math

Area-based rules ignore window-to-wall ratio, shading, appliance heat and ventilation air. They are a first pass for consumers, not engineering. When a room is unusually glazed or dense, run the HVAC Load Calculator instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many BTU do I need per square foot?

The common starting rule is 20 BTU/h per square foot, adjusted for ceiling height, sun exposure, insulation and climate. Always verify with a load calculation for a real sizing decision.

Should I add 400 BTU per occupant?

Yes for steady occupants - a person in a comfortable room emits roughly 400 BTU/h of combined sensible and latent heat. Kitchens and gyms need far more.

Does ceiling height really matter?

Yes, within reason. The rule adds 5% per additional foot above 8 ft because the conditioned volume grows. Extreme vaulted ceilings deserve a proper calculation.

Why is my result different from a contractor's quote?

Contractors account for the building envelope, duct losses, solar orientation and equipment derating. The area-based value is a first guess - treat gaps as a reason to ask questions.

Conclusion

Documented, area-based BTU numbers give homeowners a defensible starting point and a vocabulary for talking to contractors - no more guessing at unit size.
