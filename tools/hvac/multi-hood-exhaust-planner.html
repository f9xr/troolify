Demand is a scenario, not a sum

Exhaust systems sized for every hood running at full sash at once waste fan capacity and energy. The demand basis is a scenario: which hoods are realistically active together, and what stands by for the rest. Comparing connected to active flow makes that scenario explicit.

How the planner works

List each hood's exhaust CFM; the planner sums the total connection and the 'active N largest' scenario, then reports the standby margin the exhaust system must still be able to call on. Sorted largest-first means the active assumption tests the heaviest realistic combination.

Scenario is not compliance

Active-count assumptions must honour the lab's ventilation standard and chemical risk assessment - a hood that can be 'off' in a scenario can still be needed suddenly. The planner documents the choice; the EH&S review owns it.

Into the make-up air side

Exhausted air must be replaced. Take the active-total airflow into the Fume Hood Exhaust Load calculator to price the conditioning load of the make-up air.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I size a multi-hood exhaust system?

Document the connected CFM (all hoods) and a justified active scenario, then size the fan and make-up system to support the active case with standby headroom.

What is a standby margin?

The difference between total connected flow and the active scenario - capacity the exhaust plant retains for when more hoods come online.

How much CFM does a fume hood need?

At the design sash height, roughly 70-100 CFM per linear foot of hood width at the target face velocity - the hood's class and the standard decide the exact value.

Can I rely on 'box fan' makes-up air?

Only with real engineering - exhausted labs need balanced make-up air at the right temperature and humidity, which is exactly what the exhaust-load tool prices.

Conclusion

Multi-hood demand is a documented scenario. Compute connected vs active, keep the margin visible, and let the ventilation standard own the verdict.
