Why traverse, not just spot-check

A single velocity reading at the sash centre can look acceptable while the corners stall. Traversing the opening in equal-area cells - common grids of 6-20 cells depending on size - exposes the distribution that containment actually depends on.

What the summary shows

Average, minimum, maximum and span describe the distribution in one glance. Uniformity (average divided by maximum) is a compact way to flag weirdness - a hood running 90% uniform behaves very differently from one with a 50% valley in a corner.

Neutral by design

This tool sums readings and stops. Acceptance - the target average, allowed spread, and method - belongs to the governing standard for your hood class, and manufacturing a verdict here would be worse than useless.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you traverse a fume hood?

Divide the sash opening into equal-area grid cells and record a velocity in each, using an anemometer held perpendicular to the face - typically 6 to 20 cells.

What is a good uniformity score?

Healthy hoods commonly run ~90% uniformity (average/max). Lower ratios mean distribution problems worth investigating before relying on the average.

Does the tool say pass/fail?

No. It summarizes numbers and leaves the acceptance criteria to the governing standard and your workplace policy, exactly as it should.

How many readings should I take?

More is better - 6-20 cells depending on hood width and the standard's method. Paste them in any order; the summary is order-independent.

Conclusion

A traverse turns one comfortable-looking number into an honest picture of the whole opening. Summarize here and judge against the standard.
