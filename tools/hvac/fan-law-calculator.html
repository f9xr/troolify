Same-System Fan Law

Ready - fan-law scenario in this tab.

The fan laws apply only to the same fan and the same air path: system curve unchanged, density constant. Friction, damper, or duct changes invalidate them. Full disclaimer.

The three laws

For the same fan on the same system, airflow scales directly with speed, static pressure with the square of speed, and brake horsepower with the cube. A 15% speed increase therefore yields 15% more CFM, about 32% more static pressure and 52% more power - the motor load is the constraint that sneaks up on everyone.

The 'same-system' clause

These are affinity laws: they hold while the fan and air path stay fixed and density stays constant. If dampers, ducts or filters change the system curve, or the ambient air density shifts, the relationships break down.

Why horsepower is the limit

The cubic power law makes speed increases brutally expensive on the motor side. Underestimate it once - a 'small' sheave change - and the motor trips or burns. Always check the motor service factor after any speed change.

Driving the change

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the fan laws?

For one fan at constant density: CFM rises linearly with RPM, static pressure with RPM squared, and brake horsepower with RPM cubed.

Can I apply fan laws when I change the ductwork?

No - changing the system curve breaks the affinity assumption. You must re-plot on the new system curve.

How much power does a 10% speed increase need?

About 33% more (1.1 ³ = 1.331) - the cubic law again. Motor and belt limits must be rechecked.

Do the laws apply at higher altitude?

Only with a density correction. Air density falls with altitude, lowering the static pressure and horsepower the fan develops at the same speed.

Conclusion

Fan laws make speed changes predictable - and the cube law keeps them humble. Model the change here before you change a sheave.
