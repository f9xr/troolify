BMR Calculator: Know the Calories Your Body Burns at Rest

Your body burns calories even while you sleep. Its bare-minimum running costs - heartbeat, breathing, cell repair and temperature control - are called your basal metabolic rate (BMR). Knowing this number is the foundation of any sensible calorie plan, and this calculator estimates it in seconds using the respected Mifflin-St Jeor equation.

What Is Basal Metabolic Rate?

BMR is the energy your body needs at complete rest, typically 60 to 75% of your total daily energy use. It is driven largely by your lean muscle mass, which is why men and heavier people usually have higher BMRs. Older adults see their BMR gently decline as muscle mass falls.

Multiply your BMR by an activity factor and you get TDEE - your total daily energy expenditure, in other words, the calories needed just to hold your weight steady.

The Mifflin-St Jeor Equation

The calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor formula, widely regarded as the most accurate predictive equation for healthy adults:

  • Men: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age (years) + 5
  • Women: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age (years) − 161

TDEE is then BMR multiplied by your activity factor, from 1.2 (sedentary) up to 1.9 (hard training).

Turning BMR Into a Daily Plan

Once you have your maintenance calories, moving your weight is simple arithmetic. A 15% calorie cut is a sustainable way to lose fat while preserving muscle and energy, while adding about 10% supports a slow, clean gain. Frequently Asked Questions

What is basal metabolic rate (BMR)?

BMR is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest to keep vital functions running - breathing, circulation, cell repair and temperature control. It typically accounts for 60 to 75% of your total daily energy expenditure.

What is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation?

For men it is BMR = 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age (years) + 5. For women it is the same formula but with -161 instead of +5. It is widely considered one of the most accurate predictive equations for healthy adults.

How do I use BMR to estimate daily calories?

Multiply your BMR by an activity factor - about 1.2 for a sedentary day, 1.375 lightly active, 1.55 moderately active, 1.725 very active, and 1.9 for hard training. The result is your maintenance calories (TDEE).

Why is a 15% calorie cut good for mild weight loss?

A modest deficit around 15% of maintenance calories is gentle enough to preserve muscle and energy while still driving steady fat loss. Bigger, aggressive cuts are harder to sustain and more likely to be abandoned.

Conclusion
